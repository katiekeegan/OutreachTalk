Parrots, Probability, and Predictions

How a Language Model Learns to Talk

Katie Keegan · Emory MLIP

Before we simplify

Real language models learn from trillions of words.

Architecture

Transformer neural networks with billions of learned parameters — not explicit rules, but compressed statistical patterns across enormous amounts of text.

Training

Predict the next token, repeatedly, across hundreds of billions of examples. Then refine with human feedback to be more helpful and less harmful.

Scale

GPT-4, Gemini, Claude. Trained on books, code, the whole web. The training corpus shapes everything the model can and cannot say.

Every output is downstream of training data. Change the data — its scope, its balance, its gaps — and you change the model's behavior.
Framing: The simplification coming in the next two slides is deliberate. But the stakes here are real. The toy model will make the mechanism visible; the concepts transfer to systems at this scale.

Academic context · FAccT 2021

"On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots"

Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, Shmitchell

Bias at scale

Models trained on internet text inherit and amplify the biases in that data — and at scale those patterns affect millions of people.

Cost

Training very large models consumes enormous energy and financial resources that are unequally distributed.

The parrot claim

Fluent text is not evidence of understanding. A model stitches together statistically plausible sequences without comprehending meaning.

The controversy: Google pressured the authors to retract the paper. Lead researcher Timnit Gebru — known for her work on algorithmic bias — was fired. Co-lead Margaret Mitchell was fired shortly after. The case is now a reference point in debates about corporate AI governance and the suppression of safety-critical research. Karen Hao's reporting in MIT Technology Review (April 2021) is the best starting point.
For the room: The parrot claim is contested — critics argue it conflates statistical competence with the absence of something meaningful. What is not contested: the bias-at-scale argument, and the institutional controversy. Both are relevant to what we are about to do: ask an audience to build a dataset.

Pedagogical honesty

Today's model is a word counter, not a neural network.

N-gram model (today)

Counts how often each word follows a given sequence. Every probability on screen is a raw count from data you provided. No learned parameters. No embeddings. Fully transparent.

Real LLMs

Billions of parameters compress patterns from trillions of words into dense representations. They generalize in ways a word count cannot, and their internals are not directly readable.

It is a window into the logic, not a replica of the thing. Frequency, coverage, representation, context, and temperature all shape real model behavior — they are just harder to see there.
If challenged: Yes, this is a Markov model. The point is exactly that it does not generalize — you can trace every decision back to a count. Ask skeptics to compare what the toy produces with what a real system produces on the same prompt and ask what is different and why. That gap is the lesson.

P(text) = ∏ P(next token | earlier tokens)

“Autoregressive” means the model uses its own growing output as the next input.

The model does not retrieve one memorized answer. It repeatedly chooses from a distribution.
Demo idea: Have four volunteers hold cards: context, probabilities, chosen word, new context. Walk the loop physically.

Prediction after “the scientist said”

Important: Real bias is more complicated than one slider. It can enter through selection, labeling, historical inequity, filtering, objectives, and deployment choices.

Experiment 4

The same word can live in different worlds

Prompt: “The next plane is…”
Training distribution

The kinds of examples the model saw.

Deployment distribution

The situations where people actually use it.

Distribution shift

When those worlds do not match.

Ask: In geometry, what does “plane” mean? In travel? A model needs context and suitable training coverage to disambiguate.

Experiment 5

Training data shapes the menu; sampling chooses the meal

Low temperature favors likely words. High temperature flattens the distribution and increases variety.

After “math can be”:

Changing temperature does not add knowledge. It changes how boldly the model samples from what it learned.
Analogy: Training data writes the restaurant menu. Temperature changes whether you always order the bestseller or try something unusual.
