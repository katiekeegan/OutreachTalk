What will the model say next?

A language model is a probability machine. Change the examples it learns from, and you change the future it predicts.

“Autoregressive” means the model uses its own growing output as the next input.

The model does not retrieve one memorized answer. It repeatedly chooses from a distribution.
Demo idea: Have four volunteers hold cards: context, probabilities, chosen word, new context. Walk the loop physically.

Experiment 1

Frequency becomes probability

After “the shape is”:

Learned distribution

More examples usually mean more probability—unless model design, context, or data quality changes the pattern.
Audience move: Ask someone to “program” the model by moving sliders. They are programming with examples, not explicit rules.

Experiment 2

A model cannot learn examples it never sees

Training shelf

Prompt: “A Möbius strip has…”

Select the shelf, then test.
Coverage

Did the dataset contain the topic, language, style, or situation?

Quality

Were the examples accurate, clear, diverse, and appropriately labeled?

Clarify: Large datasets can still have holes. “More data” and “the right data” are different goals.

Experiment 3

Skewed examples create skewed associations

Toy training sentence:

“The scientist said he discovered a pattern.”

Prediction after “the scientist said”

This toy model is not discovering who scientists are. It is reflecting which sentence patterns were repeated.

Important: Real bias is more complicated than one slider. It can enter through selection, labeling, historical inequity, filtering, objectives, and deployment choices.
Frame carefully: The goal is not to claim every output is a direct count. The toy example isolates one mechanism: uneven representation.

Experiment 4

When those worlds do not match.

Ask: In geometry, what does “plane” mean? In travel? A model needs context and suitable training coverage to disambiguate.

Experiment 5

Training data shapes the menu; sampling chooses the meal

Low temperature favors likely words. High temperature flattens the distribution and increases variety.

After “math can be”:

Changing temperature does not add knowledge. It changes how boldly the model samples from what it learned.
Analogy: Training data writes the restaurant menu. Temperature changes whether you always order the bestseller or try something unusual.
